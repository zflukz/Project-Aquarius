\Head{Section 1}

\SubHead{1.1 Project introduce \& problem statement}
\infofont
Flooding is among the most destructive natural hazards in Thailand, causing severe
human casualties and infrastructure failure [1]. During the critical 72-hour Golden Window,
rapid survivor localization is paramount before threats of drowning and severe physical
exposure escalate [2], [3]. However, hazardous post-flood environments characterized by
high-velocity currents, submerged structural wreckage, damaged utility poles, and
communication blackouts severely obstruct boat and ground teams, forcing responders into
hazardous, slow manual blind searches [4].

While Unmanned Aerial Vehicles (UAVs) provide elevated situational awareness without
placing personnel directly into hazardous waters [5], their practical deployment in emergency
flood response is hindered by two core engineering bottlenecks:

\begin{infoenumerate}
\item \textbf{Manual Visual Observation Bottleneck:} Conventional operations rely on ground
operators manually screening live aerial video feeds on ground station monitors. This process
imposes severe cognitive fatigue and carries a high risk of target omission across visually
complex, reflective, and turbid water environments [5], [6].
\item \textbf{Georeferencing Disconnect (2D Image to 3D Physical Space):} Standard vision
models yield only 2D bounding boxes on an image plane. Without continuous geometric
transformation using flight telemetry and surface boundary constraints, visual detections
cannot be translated into actionable geographic coordinates required by field rescue vessels [6].
\end{infoenumerate}

To address these limitations, this project develops an end-to-end automated spatial
data pipeline and mission supervision platform for multi-UAV flood search operations. The
proposed system integrates fleet-size adaptive mCPP, battery-aware dynamic replanning,
AI-based survivor detection, high-frequency telemetry streaming, and ray-plane georeferencing
into a centralized tactical mapping dashboard. By autonomously filtering visual noise and
transforming aerial detections into spatial database records, the system provides real-time
geographic mapping for field commanders. This approach shifts post-flood operations from
manual blind searches to a data-directed rescue model [3].

\SubHead{1.2 Objectives}
\begin{infoenumerate}
\item To design and implement a scalable Multi-UAV Coverage Path Planning (mCPP)
system that adaptively partitions and allocates search areas across varying fleet sizes for flood
search missions.
\item To develop an adaptive dynamic replanning mechanism capable of monitoring UAV
battery states and automatically re-allocating remaining search.
\item To implement an automated survivor detection and georeferencing engine to
compute real-world geographic coordinates.
\item To develop a centralized monitoring dashboard for defining mission boundaries,
tracking fleet status and telemetry in real time, and visualizing detected survivor incidents with
image evidence and generating post mission summary analytics for tactical handover and
debriefing.
\item To evaluate overall system performance across varying fleet sizes, focusing on area
coverage efficiency, mission completion time, dynamic replanning resilience under battery
constraints, and target georeferencing accuracy under simulated environments.
\item To validate a physical proof-of-concept by deploying the developed system onto
an actual UAV platform using a standardized software interface, demonstrating reusable
integration across compatible drones equipped with onboard vision, GPS, and flight-control
capabilities under controlled test environments.
\end{infoenumerate}

\SubHead{1.3 Scope}
\SubSubHead{1.3.1 Solutions}
\infofont
This project aims to develop an end-to-end Multi-UAV system for post-flood
search and rescue (SAR). It integrates adaptive Multi-UAV Coverage Path Planning
(mCPP), real-time AI human detection, and dynamic fleet battery management to
autonomously locate stranded individuals and instantly calculate their real-world
geographic coordinates.

\SubSubHead{1.3.2 Feature}
\begin{infoitemize}
\item \textbf{Search and Coverage Path Planning (mCPP):} An automated engine that
dynamically partitions a user-defined search area into $N$ balanced, non-overlapping
trajectories based on the number of active drones.
\item \textbf{Adaptive Dynamic Replanning:} A runtime supervisor that monitors individual
drone battery states. If a unit triggers Return-to-Launch (RTL) due to power
depletion, the module automatically redistributes unvisited waypoints among the
remaining fleet to ensure 100\% coverage.
\item \textbf{AI Human Detection \& Georeferencing Engine:} Uses YOLO to detect and classify
humans from live video streams. A ray-plane intersection algorithm projects 2D
image bounding box centroids into 3D geographic coordinates using synchronized
camera and drone telemetry.
\item \textbf{Centralized Monitoring Dashboard:} A Grafana-based interface visualizing live
telemetry (battery, altitude, position). It also generates post-mission analytics,
including area coverage percentages, total duration, and a verified survivor ledger
with coordinates and snapshots.
\item \textbf{Telemetry \& Video Streaming Pipeline:} Transmits live video to a central server
alongside MQTT-based telemetry and control messages.
\item \textbf{Simulation \& Hardware Implementation:} Algorithms are rigorously tested in
Gazebo, using PX4 for flight control and ROS 2 for communication, before
deployment onto physical UAV platforms via reusable, standardized software interfaces.
\end{infoitemize}

\SubSubHead{1.3.3 Target Users and Operating Environment}
\infofont
The primary target users are tactical SAR incident commanders and UAV fleet operators
at the command post. To prevent visual fatigue from continuous aerial surveillance, the
system acts as an assistant that autonomously flags candidate targets and computes
coordinates, allowing operators to focus on rapid cross-verification rather than exhaustive
visual scanning.

The secondary target group comprises field rescue boat units; while they do not
interact with the software pipeline directly, they utilize the verified geographic coordinates
and confirmation snapshots to execute direct, point-to-point survivor extractions instead of
conducting perilous, unguided water searches.

\SubHead{2.1 Background}
\SubSubHead{2.1.1 Operational Workflows for UAVs in Flood SAR Missions}
\infofont
Search and Rescue (SAR) operations following severe flood events are highly time-sensitive,
governed by the critical 72-hour survival window where the probability of rescuing living
victims is highest [3], [4]. Post-flood environments pose severe logistical hazards for
traditional ground and boat rescue teams, including high-velocity water currents, submerged
infrastructure, debris, and downed electrical lines [2]. While deploying a single Unmanned
Aerial Vehicle (UAV) provides a safer, elevated vantage point [5], traditional manual drone
operations require operators to exhaustively monitor live video feeds. This manual approach
is unscalable for widespread disasters, as operators rapidly suffer from cognitive fatigue,
increasing the risk of missing stranded survivors amidst complex, debris-filled floodwaters [6].

\MinorHead{2.1.1.1 Multi-UAV Coordination and Aerial Fleet Constraints}
\infofont
To overcome the limitations of a single UAV, namely limited battery capacity (typically
20--30 minutes of flight time) and restricted area coverage [5], SAR operations increasingly
utilize Multi-UAV fleets. Deploying multiple drones allows for the parallelization of search
tasks, significantly reducing the time required to sweep a large region [7]. However,
multi-UAV coordination introduces strict operational constraints. The system must ensure
that drones do not overlap their search boundaries (to maximize efficiency) and do not
cross paths (to avoid collisions). Furthermore, aerial fleets are dynamic; drones consume
battery at different rates depending on wind resistance and payload. A robust multi-UAV
system must be capable of adapting on the fly, autonomously reassigning search zones if a
unit drops offline or triggers a Return-to-Launch (RTL) protocol [7].

\MinorHead{2.1.1.2 Autonomous Swarm Workflows}
\infofont
In time-critical post-flood environments, Search and Rescue (SAR) operations benefit
significantly from transitioning from manual visual searches to autonomous, multi-agent
swarm workflows [3], [4]. To efficiently scale these operations, modern systems can be
modeled on formal scenario description languages for unmanned swarms, such as SL4U [8].

As illustrated in the cooperative swarm framework (Figure 2.1), operations are divided
into distinct but interconnected phases: an autonomous aerial Search Team and a physical
Rescue Team [8]. The Search Team is deployed to rapidly scan the designated area; if no
target is found, it returns to base [8]. However, upon positive target identification, the
system immediately triggers the deployment of the Rescue Team to execute a direct,
point-to-point extraction, while the Search Team concludes its flight [8]. This multi-team
architecture provides the foundational background for replacing hazardous blind searches
with a coordinated, data-driven rescue interception.

\SubSubHead{2.1.2 Theoretical Foundations and Core Algorithms}
\MinorHead{2.1.2.1 Multi-UAV Coverage Path Planning (mCPP) algorithm}
\infofont
Coverage Path Planning (CPP) is a computational geometry problem that determines a
path passing through all points of a given area or volume while avoiding obstacles [7]. In a
Multi-UAV context (mCPP), the algorithm must first partition a user-defined global search
area into $N$ distinct sub-regions, where $N$ represents the number of active drones. This is
typically achieved using area decomposition techniques, such as grid-based partitioning or
Boustrophedon cellular decomposition, which balance the workload evenly across the fleet
[7]. Once partitioned, the algorithm generates deterministic, non-overlapping trajectories,
most commonly ``lawnmower'' or zigzag patterns, tailored to the specific geometry of each
sub-region. If the fleet size changes due to battery constraints, the mCPP algorithm must
recalculate the remaining unsearched spatial graph and dynamically re-partition the area
among the surviving drones to guarantee 100 percent geographic coverage [7].

\MinorHead{2.1.2.2 YOLO-Based Object Detection for UAVs}
\infofont
You Only Look Once (YOLO) is a state-of-the-art, single-stage object detection
architecture highly optimized for real-time computer vision [9]. Unlike two-stage detectors
(like Faster R-CNN) that first propose regions of interest and then classify them, YOLO
frames object detection as a single regression problem. It divides the input image into a
grid and simultaneously predicts bounding box coordinates and class probabilities for each
grid cell [9]. This unified architecture dramatically reduces inference latency, making YOLO
exceptionally suited for processing high-frequency live video streams transmitted from
moving UAVs [6]. In modern UAV-based SAR literature, YOLO is frequently utilized to
autonomously isolate and classify human targets from high-altitude aerial feeds, flagging
potential survivors faster than a human operator could perceive them.

\MinorHead{2.1.2.3 Projective Geometry \& Ray-Plane Georeferencing}

\textbf{\infofont Projective Geometry and Camera Calibration}\par
\infofont
Projective geometry describes the mathematical relationship between a 3D point in the
real world and its 2D projection onto an image plane. The camera projection model can be
expressed as
\begin{equation}
s\begin{bmatrix} u \\ v \\ 1 \end{bmatrix} = K \begin{bmatrix} X_c \\ Y_c \\ Z_c \end{bmatrix}
\end{equation}
where $u$ and $v$ are image pixel coordinates, $X_c, Y_c, Z_c$ are coordinates in the camera
frame, $K$ is the camera intrinsic matrix, and $s$ is a scale factor. The intrinsic matrix is
\begin{equation}
K = \begin{bmatrix} f_x & 0 & c_x \\ 0 & f_y & c_y \\ 0 & 0 & 1 \end{bmatrix}
\end{equation}
where $f_x$ and $f_y$ represent the focal lengths and $c_x, c_y$ represent the principal point.
Camera calibration provides these intrinsic parameters, which are required to determine
the viewing direction of a detected object [7], [9].

\textbf{\infofont Pixel-to-Ray Transformation}\par
\infofont
A point in an image can be represented by its pixel coordinates $(u,v)$. These coordinates
can be transformed into a 3D viewing ray in the camera coordinate system using the inverse
of the intrinsic matrix:
\begin{equation}
r_c = K^{-1}\begin{bmatrix} u \\ v \\ 1 \end{bmatrix}
\end{equation}
where $r_c$ represents the direction of the viewing ray in the camera coordinate system.
The viewing ray can then be transformed into a world coordinate system using a rotation
matrix:
\begin{equation}
r_w = R r_c
\end{equation}
where $R$ represents the rotation between the camera and world coordinate systems and
$r_w$ represents the ray direction in the world coordinate system. The position of a point
along the ray is represented as
\begin{equation}
P(t) = C + t r_w
\end{equation}
where $C$ is the camera position and $t$ represents the distance along the ray [23], [7].

\textbf{\infofont Ray-Plane Intersection}\par
\infofont
Ray-plane intersection is a geometric method for determining the position of a point by
finding the intersection between a viewing ray and a defined planar surface. A plane in
three-dimensional space can be represented by
\begin{equation}
ax + by + cz + d = 0
\end{equation}
The intersection point is obtained by determining the value of $t$ for which $P(t) = C + t r_w$
satisfies the plane equation. The resulting point represents the estimated 3D position of the
detected person on the reference surface.

For surfaces that cannot be adequately represented by a single plane, the same
principle can be extended by using a three-dimensional surface or elevation model.
Ray-based intersection with three-dimensional terrain has been applied in image-based
geolocation research [7], [9].

\textbf{\infofont Georeferencing in Post-Flood Environments}\par
\infofont
Georeferencing is the process of relating a position in an image or local coordinate
system to a geographic coordinate system. The general geometric transformation can be
represented as
\begin{equation}
(u,v) \rightarrow r_c \rightarrow r_w \rightarrow P(X,Y,Z) \rightarrow (\text{Latitude}, \text{Longitude})
\end{equation}
The accuracy of this process depends on the accuracy of the camera calibration,
coordinate transformations, camera position and orientation, and the reference surface used
for determining the three-dimensional position. These geometric principles form the basis of
image-based geolocation methods [7], [9].

\MinorHead{2.1.2.4 Distributed Data Streaming}

\textbf{\infofont Fundamentals of Distributed Message Systems}\par
\infofont
Distributed message systems enable communication between independent applications
by exchanging structured data through an intermediary message broker [10]. A foundational
architecture is the Publish/Subscribe (Pub/Sub) pattern, where publishers dispatch messages
to designated topics without direct knowledge of receiving nodes, and subscribers consume
payloads from topics of interest. This architectural pattern decouples senders and receivers
across both space and time while enabling non-blocking, asynchronous communication [10].

A critical characteristic of these systems is the enforcement of delivery guarantees,
which govern message transmission reliability [10]. Standard models include at-most-once,
where messages may be dropped during network degradation but are never duplicated;
at-least-once, which guarantees message arrival via retries at the expense of potential
duplicates; and exactly-once, which ensures each message is processed without loss or
duplication through coordinated state management [10]. These guarantees allow system
designers to establish an optimal trade-off among protocol throughput, delivery latency, and
implementation complexity [11].

\textbf{\infofont Message Queuing Telemetry Transport (MQTT) Protocol}\par
\infofont
Message Queuing Telemetry Transport (MQTT) is a lightweight messaging protocol
designed for communication in environments with limited bandwidth and constrained
devices, making it particularly suitable for Machine-to-Machine (M2M) and Internet of Things
(IoT) applications [11]. MQTT implements the publish/subscribe model through a broker,
which manages message distribution between clients. Publishers send messages to specific
topics, while subscribers receive messages by subscribing to those topics [12].

Furthermore, MQTT provides three configurable Quality of Service (QoS) levels to
balance message delivery reliability and communication overhead [11]. QoS 0 (At most
once) delivers a message without requiring an acknowledgment. QoS 1 (At least once) uses
acknowledgment and retransmission to ensure that the message is delivered, although
duplicate messages may occur. QoS 2 (Exactly once) uses a four-step handshake to ensure
that each message is delivered and processed only once [11]. These QoS levels allow
applications to select an appropriate level of delivery reliability according to their
requirements and network conditions [11].

\SubSubHead{2.1.3 System Infrastructure and Frameworks}
\MinorHead{2.1.3.1 Distributed Data Streaming Infrastructure}

\textbf{\infofont Apache Kafka Architecture and Stream Processing}\par
\infofont
Apache Kafka is a distributed event streaming platform designed to handle
high-throughput, scalable, and fault-tolerant data streams [13]. Kafka organizes events into
topics, which are divided into multiple partitions [13]. Partitions allow events to be
distributed across multiple brokers, enabling parallel data processing and horizontal
scalability [10,13]. Kafka also replicates partitions across brokers to improve fault tolerance
and maintain data availability in the event of broker failures [13].

Kafka consists of producers, brokers, and consumers [13]. Producers publish events to
Kafka topics, while consumers retrieve and process these events asynchronously [10,13].
Consumers can be organized into consumer groups, allowing multiple consumer instances
to share the processing workload of a topic's partitions [13]. This architecture enables Kafka
to efficiently handle large volumes of continuous data while allowing events to be retained
and consumed at different rates [13].

For stream processing, Kafka provides Kafka Streams, a client library for building
applications that process and analyze data continuously as events are received [14]. It
supports operations such as filtering, transformation, aggregation, joining, and windowing of
event streams [14]. Kafka Streams uses Kafka topics and partitions as the foundation for
distributed processing, enabling applications to scale while maintaining processing state and
fault tolerance [13], [14].

\MinorHead{2.1.3.2 Time-Series Databases}

\textbf{\infofont Time-Series Data Management for High-Frequency Telemetry}\par
\infofont
Time-series data consists of data points recorded sequentially over time, where each
measurement is associated with an explicit timestamp [15]. In high-frequency telemetry
systems, sensors and edge devices continuously generate large volumes of timestamped
records, including geographic coordinates, environmental metrics, and hardware operational
states [15]. Managing these data streams requires specialized database architectures, as
standard relational storage engines experience severe write degradation under
high-ingestion workloads when primary index structures exceed available physical memory
[15]. Consequently, systems designed for high-frequency telemetry prioritize high-throughput
append-only writes, automated partitioning by temporal ranges, and efficient aggregation
mechanisms over specific time windows [15].

\textbf{\infofont TimescaleDB}\par
\infofont
TimescaleDB is an open-source time-series database engineered as an extension of
PostgreSQL, combining native time-series data management capabilities with full relational
database features and standard SQL support [15], [16]. TimescaleDB introduces an
architectural abstraction known as hypertables, which present themselves to client
applications as single standard tables while internally partitioning data across two
dimensions: time intervals and optional space partitions [16]. These partitions are
materialized as isolated physical tables termed chunks [16]. By dynamically sizing chunks to
fit within system memory (RAM), TimescaleDB maintains steady high-throughput ingestion
rates without I/O thrashing, while providing native retention policies, analytical window
functions, and transparent integration with existing relational schemas [15], [16].

\MinorHead{2.1.3.3 Spatial Database}

\textbf{\infofont Relational Spatial Databases}\par
\infofont
Spatial databases are specialized data management systems designed to store,
manipulate, and query geometric and geographic entities, such as points, linestrings, and
polygons [17]. Unlike conventional alphanumeric relational schemas, spatial data models
represent entities situated within multidimensional coordinate reference systems, requiring
specialized operations to evaluate topological predicates such as containment, intersection,
adjacency, and Euclidean proximity [17]. To support these operations efficiently, spatial
database engines implement standardized spatial data types, coordinate transformation
functions, and geometric algorithms adhering to Open Geospatial Consortium (OGC)
specifications [17].

\textbf{\infofont Geospatial Indexing}\par
\infofont
Geospatial indexing structures are required to maintain deterministic query execution
times over dense geographic datasets. Conventional one-dimensional B-tree indexes cannot
preserve multidimensional spatial locality, failing to index multidimensional coordinate
spaces effectively [17], [18]. To resolve this, spatial engines implement tree-based bounding
structures, notably the Generalized Search Tree (GiST) [18]. GiST partitions coordinate
spaces hierarchically using Minimum Bounding Boxes (MBRs), executing queries through a
two-step evaluation: an initial bounding-box filter phase that swiftly discards non-candidate
geometries, followed by an exact refinement phase that executes rigorous mathematical
predicates on candidate geometries [18]. This hierarchical pruning drastically minimizes
unnecessary disk I/O and computing overhead during complex boundary intersections [18].

\textbf{\infofont PostGIS}\par
\infofont
PostGIS is an open-source spatial extension for PostgreSQL that integrates geospatial
storage, multidimensional indexing, and spatial analysis natively into a relational database
management system [19]. PostGIS introduces the geometry (planar Euclidean) and
geography (geodetic spherical) data types, providing an extensive suite of spatial functions
adhering to SQL/MM standards for distance calculation, dynamic buffer generation, and
topological union operations [17], [19]. Furthermore, PostGIS natively incorporates GiST
indexing, delivering scalable spatial query performance across large-scale vector geometries
[18], [19]. This architectural integration allows systems to query complex spatial geometries
alongside transactional attributes and time-series telemetry within a unified SQL
environment [19].

\MinorHead{2.1.3.4 Robotics Simulation and Middleware}

\textbf{\infofont 3D Physics Simulation (Gazebo Simulator)}\par
\infofont
Gazebo is a physics-based robotics simulator used to develop and evaluate robotic
systems in virtual environments. It provides simulation of robot dynamics, sensors,
environments, and interactions with simulated objects, allowing robotic algorithms to be
tested without requiring physical hardware. This is particularly useful for UAV research
because real-world flight experiments can be costly, time-consuming, and subject to safety
and hardware constraints. Previous research has demonstrated the use of Gazebo together
with ROS and PX4 to create simulation environments for UAV navigation, localization, and
path-planning research [20].

\textbf{\infofont Autopilot Firmware and Software-in-the-Loop (PX4 SITL)}\par
\infofont
PX4 Autopilot is an open-source flight-control software platform designed for
autonomous systems including unmanned aerial vehicles (UAVs). It provides the
flight-control functions required to operate a vehicle, including state estimation, control,
navigation, and actuator management. PX4 can operate on physical flight-controller hardware
as well as in simulation environments, allowing the same flight-control software to be
evaluated before deployment on a real UAV [21].

Software-in-the-Loop (SITL) is a simulation approach in which the PX4 flight stack
executes on a computer instead of a physical flight controller. The simulated vehicle
provides sensor information to PX4, while PX4 processes this information and generates
control and actuator outputs for the simulated vehicle. This allows flight-control software,
navigation algorithms, and mission behavior to be tested without requiring physical UAV
hardware [21], [22].

\textbf{\infofont Robot Operating System (ROS 2)}\par
\infofont
Robot Operating System 2 (ROS 2) is a middleware framework designed to facilitate the
development of distributed robotic applications. It provides a structured communication
architecture through which independent software components can exchange data and
coordinate robotic functions. ROS 2 was designed to address limitations of earlier robotic
middleware and to support applications requiring distributed computation, real-time
capabilities, and operation across different robotic platforms [23].

\textbf{\infofont MAVLink Protocol}\par
\infofont
Micro Air Vehicle Link (MAVLink) is a lightweight communication protocol designed for
communication between unmanned systems and external components such as ground
control stations. It defines a set of messages for exchanging information related to vehicle
operation, telemetry, and mission control. MAVLink has been adopted by major open-source
autopilot platforms, including PX4 and ArduPilot, making it widely used in unmanned aerial
vehicle (UAV) systems [24].

\MinorHead{2.1.3.5 Centralized Observability and Dashboard Visualization with Grafana}
\infofont
Grafana is an open-source visualization and monitoring platform that can be used to
present time-series and other monitoring data through configurable dashboards. It can be
integrated with data sources such as Prometheus, allowing collected metrics to be queried
and presented through graphs, tables, and other visualizations [25].

\SubSubHead{2.1.4 Existing Solutions}
\infofont
Recent advancements in Unmanned Aerial Vehicles (UAVs) have led to the development
of autonomous systems designed to replace manual search and rescue operations. For
instance, in maritime environments, autonomous UAV-based systems have been proposed
to assist in time-critical Man Overboard (MOB) incidents [26]. These systems utilize onboard
cameras and flight path computations to algorithmically detect humans at sea, significantly
reducing the time-to-target compared to traditional coastguard vessels. However, these
solutions are primarily optimized for open-water maritime environments using single-agent
deployments, which lack the multi-UAV coordination required to rapidly cover massive,
fragmented post-flood disaster zones.

Furthermore, contemporary research into autonomous drone platforms continues to
optimize AI-based detection and cooperative search algorithms [27]. While these existing
solutions successfully demonstrate the viability of airborne AI detection, they often overlook
the specific operational constraints of flood environments -- namely, managing dynamic
battery depletion across a multi-drone fleet and the complex geometric georeferencing
required when navigating over uneven, debris-filled submerged terrains.

Based on the study of Multi-UAV systems, PX4, ROS 2, MQTT, Kafka, computer vision,

Field and Edge, Gateway, Kafka Streaming, Processing and Decision, Data Persistence, and

Each UAV sends telemetry data and image frames through MQTT to the Gateway
Layer, where the data is forwarded to Kafka for further processing. Image frames are

information is then visualized through Grafana using both real-time and mission summary
dashboards.

processed, and analyzed to generate updated mission commands that can be sent back to
the UAV fleet during the mission.
